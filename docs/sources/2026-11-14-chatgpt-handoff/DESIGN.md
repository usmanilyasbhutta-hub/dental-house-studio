## Art-direction layer — mandatory before layout
The governing creative sequence is:

**Context → campaign DNA → DHS anchor → concept → references → hero → typography → authentic proof → campaign styling → commercial layer → subtraction → category check → DHS check → quality gate**

This replaces the weaker default of **content → layout → polish**. Detailed craft rules later in this file are execution tools, not the starting point.

### Brand invariant
**DHS identity is the base layer; campaign styling is a temporary skin.** A campaign may change mood, imagery, typography emphasis, props, pacing or cultural context, but it must not replace the underlying DHS visual behaviour. Campaign direction may evolve aggressively; DHS identity must remain recognisable.

### 1. Define the campaign world
Before layout, define the audience moment, emotional context, visual mood, perceived value and what the piece should feel like before it explains anything. Do not begin by arranging price, package inclusions or generic headings.

#### Campaign DNA clarification — ask 4–6 questions when the campaign is new, renewed or materially unclear
If the existing brief already answers a question, infer it and do not ask again. Ask only what is genuinely unresolved. The purpose is to understand the campaign at the **idea level**, not to collect literal prompt words. Answers are semantic constraints and creative intent; they are **not mandatory words, objects, colours or motifs to reproduce literally**. Translate the idea into the strongest visual solution while preserving its meaning.

Use 4–6 of these questions as needed:
1. **What real moment are we entering?** What is happening in the patient's life, decision or context that makes this campaign relevant now?
2. **What should the viewer feel or understand before reading the details?** Define the intended emotional/mental state, not a style adjective alone.
3. **What is the single most important change we want in the viewer?** For example: notice, understand, trust, compare, save, enquire or book—but treat these as outcomes, not compulsory CTA wording.
4. **What must remain unmistakably DHS in this campaign?** Identify the strongest brand anchor: authentic clinical evidence, clinic environment, crop language, typography behaviour, colour restraint, motion grammar, tone, or another real DHS characteristic.
5. **What outside world may this campaign borrow from—and what must it never become?** This defines useful cultural/seasonal/editorial references while setting the drift boundary.
6. **What real proof or material makes the promise believable?** Identify the genuine case, procedure, clinician, equipment, environment, process or other evidence available.

The answers define a **creative territory**, not a visual checklist. Do not force every answer into the frame.

### 2. Choose the DHS anchor before campaign styling
Every campaign must establish at least one strong DHS anchor before decorative or seasonal styling is developed. The anchor may be authentic clinical imagery, genuine DHS environment, recurring crop/composition logic, DHS typography behaviour, brand colour restraint, recognizable motion grammar, or another established DHS behaviour. A logo alone does not satisfy this requirement.

### 3. Choose one strong visual concept
Use one dominant visual idea that can be described in one sentence. If the concept needs several unrelated tricks to feel interesting, simplify or renew it.

### 4. Establish reference direction
Set boundaries for composition, typography, photography/imagery treatment, tonal behaviour and—when relevant—motion. References guide taste; they do not authorize copying another brand. AI and production tools execute inside this art-directed box rather than inventing the visual language from scratch.

### 5. Build the hero first
Decide what dominates before arranging supporting information. Preferred hierarchy:

**Hero visual / hook → core message → proof → offer → details**

Do not automatically default to **headline → paragraph → icons → rounded card → price → CTA**.

### 6. Real-media-first rule and authentic proof
For DHS marketing, **real DHS footage and photography are the default source material** whenever suitable media exists. Do not replace usable real footage with generated scenes merely because generation is easier or more polished. The production task is to make authentic material presentable through strong direction and editing.

Real media may be improved through:
- decisive cropping and reframing,
- exposure, white-balance and restrained colour correction,
- stabilization and selective speed changes where appropriate,
- noise/audio cleanup and intelligible sound design,
- masking, tracking and compositing that do not change clinical truth,
- editorial typography, captions and kinetic type,
- purposeful sequencing, cutaways, match cuts and motivated transitions,
- background cleanup or scene simplification that does not fabricate the clinical event,
- subtle depth, texture and grading that preserve the actual appearance of teeth, tissue, equipment and clinic space.

The standard is not “raw = authentic.” The standard is **real source → professionally directed and edited presentation → factual integrity preserved**.

Where trust or treatment outcome is involved, use genuine DHS clinic imagery, procedures, clinicians, equipment and consented case evidence. **Generated proof is prohibited.** Never generate, regenerate, beautify or materially alter a patient result, before/after, clinical finding, testimonial, clinician action, equipment capability or clinic event and present it as evidence.

#### Match the asset to the message
Do not apply the real-media preference mechanically to every slide or frame. **Use the real asset whose role best matches the message, and do not repeat authentic media merely to prove authenticity.**

Assign each major real asset a clear job:
- **case/result = proof,**
- **DHS logo/signage = identity,**
- **treatment/process detail = process,**
- **clinic interior/exterior = place,**
- **clinician/patient interaction = human trust/context.**

The **first real asset** in a carousel or Reel should usually be the **highest-value available evidence or DHS brand cue for that specific message**, not automatically a clinic interior. If proof is the hook, use the actual case/result. If brand recognition is the hook, a strong DHS logo/signage photograph may lead. If the message is location or arrival — e.g. “Yahin milte hain” — a clinic interior is meaningful. A principle such as “Pehle assessment” does **not** require a clinic interior unless the image itself adds diagnostic or contextual meaning.

**Real-media economy rule:** prefer fewer, stronger authentic assets over repeated medium-value clinic photographs. Before reusing a clinic/photo asset, ask: **What new job does this image perform that the previous real asset did not?** If the answer is unclear, replace it with stronger typography, campaign-world design, an educational visual, process detail, or whitespace.

In short: **match the asset to the message, not the asset policy to every slide. Real where it proves; designed/illustrative where it frames; repetition only when it adds new information.**

#### Sequence-role architecture — each frame earns a different job
A carousel or Reel sequence should be planned as a progression of **communication roles**, not as repeated layouts with different copy. Not every slide/frame needs photography, and not every authentic asset belongs everywhere. A strong sequence may move through roles such as **identity → package/idea → proof → principle → place → action**, but this is an example of role logic, not a compulsory six-slide template. Change the order and number of roles to suit the campaign.

Rules:
- **One primary job per frame.** A frame may support secondary information, but its main purpose should be obvious at phone size.
- **Do not add media to a principle just to fill space.** If a statement such as “Pehle assessment” is strongest as typography, hierarchy and whitespace, let the message own the frame.
- **Use place imagery only when place is part of the message.** Clinic interiors/exteriors are most useful for arrival, familiarity, environment, location or “Yahin milte hain” style communication—not as generic filler behind unrelated principles.
- **Let proof carry the most documentary weight.** A real case/result may be the most visibly photographic/clinical frame in the sequence; surrounding frames do not need to imitate that density.
- **Typography-led frames are valid finished frames.** Add an icon, object crop, generated educational element or process cue only when it clarifies, differentiates or improves rhythm—not because an empty area feels unfinished.
- **Vary information density intentionally.** Dense proof/offer frames can be followed by quieter principle or atmosphere frames. Consistency comes from campaign world, type roles, colour behaviour, spacing and tone—not identical media density.
- **Cover/hero discipline:** when the opening frame contains a strong DHS identity cue or proof image, do not let price, discount, inclusions, validity and disclaimers all compete at equal strength. Preserve one dominant entry point and subordinate the commercial stack.
- **CTA discipline:** the final frame should make the action unmistakable first (e.g. WhatsApp keyword/booking instruction), contact route second, and price/supporting terms third unless the campaign objective is explicitly price-first.
- **Redundancy test across the whole sequence:** before adding an image, ask whether the sequence already contains another asset doing the same job. If yes, either give the new asset a clearly different role or omit it.

**Sequence test:** when thumbnails are viewed together, the set should feel like one DHS campaign with purposeful progression—not six posters that happen to share a background.

#### Generative media — educational and previsualization role only
Use the strongest current image-generation/editing capability available at production time only for:
- educational illustrations, conceptual anatomy, simplified mechanisms and explanatory visuals where generation is clinically appropriate,
- non-documentary diagrams or scene metaphors that make a concept easier to understand,
- internal art-direction exploration, storyboarding, reference frames and previsualization before shooting/editing real assets.

Generated educational material must be clearly illustrative when a viewer could otherwise mistake it for a real patient, real procedure or real DHS event. Clinical/anatomical details must be checked before publication.

Do **not** use generated people, generated treatment scenes, generated clinic interiors, generated before/after imagery or generated “proof-like” material as substitutes for authentic DHS media in final marketing unless the owner explicitly authorizes a clearly non-documentary creative concept for that specific task. Even then, it cannot function as proof.

**Image-model benchmark rule:** high-quality generation may still be used privately as a concept benchmark. If a generated frame has stronger composition, hierarchy, depth, crop, material or mood than the current edit, extract those principles and reproduce them with real DHS footage/photography wherever the final asset represents the clinic, treatment or patient reality. Do not publish a generated frame merely because it looks better than the real source.

### 7. Apply campaign styling after DHS is established
Seasonal, cultural, lifestyle or editorial elements may enrich the campaign, but they must remain subordinate to the DHS identity. Wedding cues, festive motifs, props, textures or external aesthetics should contextualize DHS, not replace it.

#### Campaign environment and cross-slide continuity
When a carousel or sequence intentionally uses one background world, **continuity is a design asset, not a flaw**. If the owner explicitly asks for a continuous background, preserve that decision unless a factual, accessibility or production problem requires change. Do not break continuity merely to create visual variety.

Continuity may be:
- **spatial:** one panoramic/extended environment continues across adjacent slides,
- **material:** the same fabric, light, architecture, surface or texture behaves consistently,
- **narrative:** the same scene evolves as the viewer advances,
- **graphic:** shapes, lines, crops or objects cross slide boundaries,
- **motion-led:** a visual vector or action continues across Reel cuts/frames.

A continuous campaign world should fuse the campaign context with DHS rather than become generic decoration. For example, a wedding campaign can create a **DHS wedding-celebration environment**—DHS space, clinical cues, brand behaviour and authentic proof enriched by wedding-season atmosphere—rather than generic wedding stationery with dental copy added later.

**Continuity does not require identical slides.** Keep the environment coherent while changing the focal crop, depth, subject, scale, information density, proof asset or interaction from frame to frame. If the background feels generic, improve the **background world itself**; do not automatically remove continuity.

Generated or staged background worlds must be treated as campaign illustration. They must not imply that a fictional celebration, patient interaction or generated scene is documentary evidence of an actual DHS event.

### 8. Add the commercial layer after the concept works
Price, discount, inclusions, validity, booking information and CTA support the creative. They do not dictate the visual concept unless the brief itself is explicitly price-led.

### 9. Subtraction pass
Remove elements that do not strengthen the concept: purposeless cards, floating icons, random rounded rectangles, decorative lines, unnecessary badges, filler copy, fake 3D dental objects, generic stock smiles, excessive effects and transitions. Whitespace is not unfinished space.

### 10. Category-recognition check
Mentally remove the logo and campaign copy. The creative should still plausibly belong to a premium clinical/dental brand. If it primarily reads as fashion, jewellery, wedding stationery, skincare, hospitality or generic lifestyle advertising, the campaign styling has overrun the category identity.

### 11. DHS-without-logo check
Mentally remove the logo. Ask: **Could this convincingly belong to another dentist, dermatologist, salon or generic Canva brand?** If yes, strengthen DHS-specific crop language, typography behaviour, authentic clinical evidence, composition logic, colour restraint, motion grammar or tone. Recognition should come from visual behaviour, not repeated logo stamping.

### 12. Preserve / Modify / Renew
- **Preserve:** concept is strong; tighten crop, spacing, hierarchy, typography or proof placement.
- **Modify:** underlying idea is good but one layer is generic or weak; change that layer.
- **Renew:** concept itself is limiting quality; rebuild rather than polishing a weak template.

Do not preserve a weak idea because work has already been invested in it.

### 13. No-AI-slop gate
Reject or rework a piece when several of these appear together: excessive glow/gradients, floating UI cards, generic giant sans headlines, decorative icons without function, stock-looking smiles, fake dental 3D, perfect template symmetry, too many hierarchy levels, every empty area filled, multiple unrelated effects, clichéd luxury motifs, or synthetic imagery replacing real proof.

**Preferred baseline:** authentic DHS footage/photography + professional editing/art direction + decisive crop + typography + whitespace + one visual idea. Generated material is primarily educational or previsualization support; it does not replace real DHS proof or usable real media.

### 14. Two-axis identity and quality gate
Approve only when all six are defensible:
- **Concept:** one clear visual idea.
- **Hierarchy:** intended viewing order is obvious.
- **Taste:** restrained, contemporary and intentional.
- **Authenticity:** evidence is credible and appropriately real.
- **Category identity:** it still feels like premium clinical/dental communication.
- **DHS identity:** it feels specifically like Dental House Sialkot rather than merely a premium dentist.

If one layer is weak, repair that layer. If several are weak, renew the concept.

## Visual principles
1. **Authentic over synthetic** — real clinic architecture, clinicians, hands, instruments, radiographs, chairside moments and real cases are preferred.
2. **Editorial hierarchy** — one dominant idea per frame/slide; strong scale contrast; deliberate whitespace.
3. **Cinematic restraint** — controlled contrast, subtle depth, restrained grain where appropriate, no horror-like darkness.
4. **Premium simplicity** — fewer elements, better spacing. Avoid decorative clutter.
5. **Clinical truth** — educational visuals should be medically plausible and never imply outcomes not supported by the case.
6. **Brand recognition without logo dependence** — the post should feel like DHS before the logo appears.

## Typography
- Prefer refined grotesk / humanist sans for most social content.
- Use an editorial serif only when it strengthens hierarchy and stays readable on mobile.
- Avoid generic default-looking typography, faux-luxury thin scripts, condensed shouty fonts and novelty type.
- Captions/subtitles: large enough for mobile, high contrast, short line lengths, no karaoke-style visual clutter unless it serves the concept.

## Layout
- Mobile-first.
- Favor asymmetric/editorial compositions over centered template stacks.
- Use large image areas and decisive crops.
- Never make every slide/frame a boxed card.
- Keep one clear focal point.

## Logo
- Do not stamp the logo on every Reel frame or carousel slide.
- Good placements: opening identity moment, final frame, occasional anchor frame, discreet watermark only when genuinely needed.
- Never distort, recolor arbitrarily, add glow, fake bevel, drop shadow or rotate the logo.

## Photography and video source priority
Preferred source order:
1. real Dental House footage/video,
2. real Dental House photography,
3. consented real case imagery and genuine clinician workflow,
4. accurate diagrams/educational illustration where the idea is better explained than filmed,
5. generated educational visuals only when useful and clearly non-documentary.

For real footage and photography, **edit for presentation rather than abandoning the source**: improve crop, pacing, lighting balance, sound, sequencing, typography integration and motion treatment while preserving factual appearance.

Avoid:
- replacing usable authentic footage with synthetic campaign scenes,
- generic smiling-stock-patient photography,
- over-whitened or generated proof-like teeth,
- impossible dental anatomy,
- fake futuristic clinics,
- grading that changes clinical findings,
- excessive teal-orange grading.

## Motion language
- **Begin with the real footage.** Identify the strongest physical action, expression, reveal, detail, camera move or sound already present before inventing motion graphics.
- Motion should clarify hierarchy, create anticipation, continue physical movement, reveal information or deliver payoff.
- Prefer **visual interruption → hard cut → micro-movement → match/motivated cut → sound cue → pattern break → payoff** when the footage supports it.
- Useful vocabulary: hard cuts, restrained 2–6 frame flash cuts, subtle push-ins, macro crops, light speed ramps, masked reveals, naturally motivated object wipes, sound-led cuts, editorial text animation, kinetic typography and occasional freeze-frame annotation.
- Use kinetic type as a **meaning-and-rhythm layer**, not decoration: type may enter, scale, track, mask, attach to movement or transform when it reinforces the spoken/visual beat. Keep it readable and subordinate to authentic footage when footage is the proof.
- Create transitions from footage whenever possible: lens occlusion, hand/object wipe, matched camera vector, light flash, push-in continuation or action match.
- Prefer physical-feeling movement, masked reveals, parallax used lightly, elegant text entrances and motivated transitions.
- Avoid transition packs, excessive zooms, random spins, page flips, neon streaks, generic zoom-whoosh effects, bounce-everything animation and constant motion.
- Use one coherent motion idea per sequence; do not add movement merely to prove that editing occurred.
- When real footage initially looks ordinary, first improve **selection, trim points, crop, pace, sound and typography integration** before deciding that the footage itself is inadequate.

## Motion direction before animation
For Reels, Stories and motion-led posts, define a **motion language before adding animation**. Motion style should not be improvised clip by clip.

Set only what the concept needs:
- **tone:** calm ↔ kinetic and soft ↔ sharp,
- **timing scale:** a small family of fast / medium / slow behaviours rather than unrelated durations,
- **easing behaviour:** one coherent physical-feeling family unless a deliberate contrast is part of the idea,
- **transition family:** hard cut, motivated mask/wipe, match/action cut, light/occlusion, or another concept-led family,
- **stagger rhythm:** character / word / line / object sequencing only when it improves hierarchy or rhythm,
- **motion intensity:** decide which moments deserve energy and which should remain still.

### Motion hierarchy
Classify moving elements as:
- **Hero:** the one movement/event that should win attention,
- **Support:** motion that clarifies sequence, meaning or hierarchy,
- **Texture:** subtle atmosphere only.

Do not let support or texture compete with the hero. Good motion direction includes deciding **what does not move**.

### Kinetic-type rule
Kinetic typography is a vocabulary, not a default style. Use word/line stagger, masking, scale, tracking, weight change, path movement or beat-synced emphasis only when it:
1. clarifies meaning,
2. reinforces rhythm, or
3. strengthens hierarchy.

If the same message reads better as stable type, keep it stable. When real footage is the proof, typography remains subordinate to the footage.

## Creative critique and cold-review gate
Judge the **output**, not merely whether a checklist was followed. A rule-compliant piece that still feels generic, dated, confused or unconvincing fails.

Before final handoff, review the actual output using the applicable tests:
- **2-second first impression:** what wins attention immediately, and is that the intended thing?
- **phone-size test:** hierarchy, text and proof must work at realistic viewing size.
- **without-logo test:** does the visual behaviour still feel like DHS and remain clearly clinical/dental?
- **thumbnail/contact-sheet test:** does a carousel/Reel show purposeful progression rather than repeated compositions?
- **truth/source test:** real proof remains real; illustrative/generative material cannot masquerade as documentary evidence.
- **asset-role test:** each major image/clip has a new job; repeated clinic imagery or repeated visual devices must add new information.
- **mute/sound test for video:** the visual story should still make sense muted; sound should add timing, texture, anticipation or payoff when heard.
- **restraint test:** identify motion, effects or decorative elements that can be removed without weakening the concept.

### Diagnose before revising
Tag the main failure before editing:
**Concept / Hierarchy / DHS identity / Category identity / Asset role / Typography / Motion / Pacing / Sound / Proof / CTA.**

Fix the highest-impact layer first. Do not respond to a hierarchy problem by adding more effects, or to weak footage by automatically replacing it with synthetic media.

Use **Preserve / Modify / Renew**:
- preserve strong approved layers,
- modify the diagnosed weak layer,
- renew only when the underlying concept is the problem.

### Learning rule
A single successful or attractive execution is **not automatically a new brand rule**. Promote a pattern into this DESIGN master only when it is repeatedly useful across different briefs, clearly strengthens DHS identity/communication, or the owner explicitly adopts it as a durable rule. Keep one-off experiments in campaign records or the creative-loop notes instead of bloating the master.

## Overall test
If the piece looks like a Canva template, generic dental ad, AI demo, wedding-video transition pack, or an influencer trend with a DHS logo pasted on it, it fails.
## Main graphic-design guide — consolidated 6 October 2026

### Authority and philosophy
This DESIGN.md is the single main graphic-design guide for DHS. It consolidates the earlier Maxwell synthesis, the supplementary design addendum and the supplied screenshot lessons. The research ledger preserves individual sources and repeated posts; production uses this deduplicated guide. Earlier documents are references, not competing instructions.

Design helps patients notice, understand and act. Use clarity, purposeful hierarchy, careful craft and recognizable DHS identity throughout. Hormozi-inspired strategy governs the patient-relevant hook, useful payoff and proportionate CTA; Maxwell-informed craft governs visual execution.

**Apply specific rules as and when required.** Choose a technique because it solves a concrete problem. Do not force every technique, a second font, fixed color percentages, shadows, faces, grids or silhouettes into every asset. Clinical truth, patient privacy, the owner's faceless preference and the permanent brand override any example.

### 1. Attention and hierarchy
**Use when:** the viewing order is unclear or several elements compete.
- Establish one dominant entry point. Rank useful explanation next, then quiet supporting detail and brand/CTA.
- Max's hierarchy caption suggests one large element, one or two medium elements and smaller supporting elements. Use this as a starting structure, not a compulsory element count.
- One focal point, hierarchy, contrast and isolation are related tools, not four separate effects to add.
- Isolate a priority through size, shape, weight, color or placement when it gets lost among similar elements. Preserve readability of the rest; do not simply fade clinically important text.
- Keep each slide useful: a relevant hook, recognizable example, explanation/evidence, practical takeaway or clear next step. Use the number of slides the message needs.
- Reduce competing actions. One proportionate CTA usually suffices. Max's Hick's-law example is a cue to reduce unnecessary choice, not a rule requiring three packages or withholding treatment options.
- Arrange reading flow for the script: English/Roman Urdu generally left to right; Urdu right to left. A top-left hook can suit an LTR composition, but is not a universal eye-tracking law.

**DHS example:** a dominant question, one explanatory diagram and a quiet takeaway; a final booking frame with one next step. Do not make every frame a sales prompt.

### 2. Typography and reading
**Use when:** copy feels cluttered, difficult to scan or visually uneven.
- Prefer one coherent type family; use a second only for a clear role. Around two families is a practical ceiling. Vary weights and styles deliberately.
- Use readable sentence case for longer headlines, explanations and subtitles. All caps may work for short labels. Max's five-to-six-word threshold is a heuristic.
- Keep lines manageable at actual phone size. Break at meaningful phrases; preserve qualifiers and Roman Urdu meaning. Do not force seven or four words per line.
- Separate headline, supporting text and quiet labels through meaningful scale/weight/spacing, not arbitrary font changes.
- Inspect letter spacing in prominent headlines. Adjust kerning selectively when a pair looks uneven; avoid excessive tracking in body copy. Do not modify spacing inside the supplied logo.
- Review actual reading size rather than a zoomed editor view. No fixed font-size number guarantees readability across fonts, scripts and platforms.

**Evidence boundary:** Max's 13% reading-speed figure, unspecified studies, and claims that an optimal line length changed from seven to four words are unverified. Retain the practical readability lesson without presenting those numbers as established research.

### 3. Color and accessible contrast
**Use when:** palette roles are unclear, emphasis is weak or text merges with its surroundings.
- Assign colors roles: dominant field, supporting content and restrained emphasis. Use DHS charcoal green, dark olive, ivory/bone and appropriate derived tones.
- Max's 60/30/10 advice and the basic-color-system frames both describe controlled dominance and accent use. They are alternative heuristics, not simultaneous required formulas.
- Monochromatic variation and analogous hues can help a palette cohere. Any adaptation stays within the approved DHS identity; the orange/red/yellow example is not a new DHS palette.
- Test text and graphic colors on their actual background/photo. The same color may appear different in another context. Do not recolor the master logo to compensate.
- Prioritize readable light/dark contrast. The two dark logo colors alone may not provide enough separation. Pastel styling never excuses illegible text.
- Do not communicate meaning through color alone: use labels, shape, symbols or numbers for comparisons and choices. Check that key content remains understandable without hue distinction.
- Use photo grading to unify assets while preserving clinical appearance. Avoid extreme contrast or artificial dental findings.

**Evidence boundary:** the visible palette frames are partial video evidence. Labels such as greater-than-10% primary and less-than-90% neutral do not establish a universal percentage. The cropped color-context caption supports background testing, but its missing beginning has not been reconstructed. The caption's color-vision population figure and stop-sign example are not adopted as factual teaching content.

### 4. Composition, grouping and optical craft
**Use when:** rows, numbers, text, photos or shapes feel disconnected or awkward.
- Start with common edges, baselines and consistent gaps. For numbered options, give numbers a shared column, align text starts and keep repeatable number-to-text spacing.
- Use column grids when multiple elements need organization. Grids are scaffolding, not visible boxes on every slide. Vary composition while preserving selected anchors.
- Group related elements closely; separate unrelated groups more clearly. Headline and explanation should visibly belong together.
- Follow technical alignment with optical inspection. Unequal visual mass may require a small adjustment even when bounding boxes match.
- Overshoot round forms only when they appear smaller beside angular forms. Max's approximately 5% example is a starting experiment, not a universal correction.
- Repeat selected colors, styles, shapes or alignment anchors to create rhythm. Preserve variation in photographs, scale and pacing. When a continuous background/world is an explicit campaign decision, continuity may span multiple slides as one environment; vary each slide's focal job rather than forcing a different background.
- If a nonessential object approaches an edge ambiguously, give it clear breathing room or crop it decisively. Max calls this the anchor effect; it is an edge-composition lesson here, not psychological price anchoring.
- Creative negative space can form a meaningful silhouette or second image when it helps the concept. Breathing room remains useful independently. Do not force a tooth silhouette or modify the logo to demonstrate the technique.

**DHS example:** shared number/text columns in a question carousel, a large clinic photograph on an occasional slide, and consistent quiet labels across the sequence.

### 5. Imagery, depth and logo treatment
**Use when:** the subject needs evidence, human context, separation or purposeful depth.
- Prefer real clinic spaces, equipment, hands, workflow and consented clinical images. Protect patient identity. Do not require the owner on camera.
- Max's face-priority advice is optional. A consented relevant human image may help some briefs; it is not necessary for every asset and does not override faceless production. Do not use generic smiling faces simply to comply.
- Integrate photography with deliberate crop, scale and lighting. Accurate flat diagrams are valid; dimensional design is not automatically better.
- Add shadows only when they help separation or spatial meaning. Prefer controlled blur, lower opacity and a suitable color tint, with coherent light direction. No compulsory shadow settings; no shadow added to the master logo.

- Max's horizontal-lockup construction method—wordmark divided into four units, one unit added above/below, a one- or two-unit gap and optical refinement—is reference material only for a future explicitly authorized new lockup. It never authorizes changing the current master.

### 6. Motion and production
- Animate the intended viewing order: reveal the hook, hold the useful explanation, then reveal the next step.
- Use one coherent motion idea per sequence. Keep text stable long enough to read. Avoid animating every element, gratuitous zooms and transition packs.
- Build recognition through recurring type roles, palette, composition and authentic photography, with restrained logo placement.
- Canva/Adobe/Higgsfield are production tools; the guide applies across them. Use the current DHS social skills and standards, not retired web-design skills.
- Keep the established carousel default at 1080 × 1350. A 1080 × 1440 alternative is conditional on composition and actual publishing-route preview. Keep all slides in one asset consistent.
- Stories/Reels use the agreed vertical production format and current route previews. Do not treat screenshots' fixed margins as official universal safe zones.
- Inspect phone-size feed, story/reel UI and grid/cover crops before handoff. Export appropriate high-quality stills/video; provide ordering, caption and supporting posting notes.
